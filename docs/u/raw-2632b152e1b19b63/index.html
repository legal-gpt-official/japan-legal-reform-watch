Background & Purpose

The Building Sanitation Act already requires persons with authority over specified buildings to maintain a sanitary environment, including supplying drinking and other relevant water that meets applicable standards when facilities provide water for human consumption. The Enforcement Regulations set inspection items by reference to the water-quality standards. A change to the drinking-water standards may leave a mismatch if the building-specific inspection list is not updated consistently.

Japan treated perfluorooctanesulfonic acid (PFOS) and perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA), persistent organic fluorine compounds, as water-quality management targets before placing the combined concentration under a binding drinking-water standard. The Environment Ministry's June 2025 amendment introduced a combined 50 ng/L standard, effective from 1 April 2026, after scientific review and consultation. That established obligation for regulated water suppliers is different from the additional building-specific periodic-inspection proposal.

The September 2026 MHLW draft would amend Article 4(1)(iv)(d) of the Building Sanitation Act Enforcement Regulations to add PFOS and PFOA to water-inspection items tested once every three years for covered specified buildings. The stated policy is consistency between the building-sanitation inspection regime and the updated drinking-water quality regime, not a finding that PFOS/PFOA exposure is universal in all buildings. The proposal does not impose a general triennial test on every home or commercial premises.

Legislative & Regulatory History

Japan's building-sanitation regime and water-supply regulation pursue related but legally distinct goals: protection of water quality at the supplier stage and hygienic maintenance within covered buildings. Earlier PFOS/PFOA management used provisional targets; after the Food Safety Commission's 2024 health-risk assessment and environmental regulatory deliberations, the Environment Ministry promulgated a revised drinking-water standard on 30 June 2025, effective 1 April 2026, with a combined 50 ng/L limit.

MHLW held a Living Environment Subcommittee meeting on 15 September 2026 discussing PFAS responses under the building-sanitation regime. On 16 September 2026 it opened e-Gov consultation 495260188 proposing to align the building-regulation periodic-test list with the new water-quality standards. That consultation formally closed at 00:00 Japan time on 16 October 2026.

The one-page official draft summary estimated promulgation during November 2026 and commencement in May 2027, described as six months after promulgation. Those are forecasts in the consultation document, not proof of enacted commencement. The e-Gov building-sanitation ordinance revision history examined on 11 November 2026 does not yet identify this new PFOS/PFOA testing amendment as promulgated.

What Happens Next

Confirmed next steps

  • 2026-10-16 — Public consultation 495260188 closed at 00:00 Japan time; the comment period is over. [1]

Expected procedural steps

The usual procedure for this kind of instrument; not a forecast for this reform.

  • MHLW may consider public submissions and finalize a ministerial ordinance. Its September draft anticipated promulgation in November 2026 and commencement in May 2027, six months after promulgation, but those dates require confirmation in the final official text.
  • Owners and building sanitation managers should update water-inspection plans only in accordance with the enacted scope and transitional provisions, while maintaining already-applicable hygiene and drinking-water duties.

Outstanding uncertainties

  • The precise promulgation date, effective date, final scope, sampling methodology and transitional arrangements for specified buildings have not been established by the cited draft consultation alone.
  • The application of periodic tests to an individual building depends on whether it falls within the statutory specified-building category and on the type of water-supply installation; it should not be assumed for every premises.
  • The water-supply regulation's own PFOS/PFOA inspection frequency and the proposed building-sanitation three-year inspection are separate obligations and should not be treated as interchangeable.

Business Impact & Practical Considerations

Managers of regulated specified buildings may need to add PFOS/PFOA to recurring water-quality testing programmes once the draft is finalized. Contracted building-maintenance operators and laboratories should check sampling, certified analytical methods, test scheduling, reporting and records in light of the final regulation. The draft specifies a three-year inspection interval, but it does not by itself establish additional building-specific thresholds beyond the cross-referenced water-quality standard.

Property owners should distinguish what is binding already under the existing building-sanitation framework, what became binding on regulated water suppliers under the April 2026 drinking-water standard, and the separate proposed inspection schedule for building owners. Prematurely claiming the proposal has been implemented can generate inaccurate vendor instructions and compliance records; ignoring the existing potable-water standards can create a different compliance risk.

Points to check

  • Determine whether each managed property is a legally specified building and which drinking-water supply facilities are subject to the building-sanitation regulations.
  • Retain existing required water testing and hygiene controls; plan for potential PFOS/PFOA tests once every three years but mark this as a draft requirement until promulgation.
  • Review the Environment Ministry's existing combined 50 ng/L drinking-water standard separately from the frequency and scope of building-level checks.
  • Monitor the final MHLW ordinance, commencement and transition rules, then update inspection contracts, laboratory scope and records based on the adopted provisions.
