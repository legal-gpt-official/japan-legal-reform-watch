Background & Purpose

The original 2022 Economic Security Promotion Act established four main tools: support for designated critical goods, critical-infrastructure equipment screening, support for advanced technologies, and protection of sensitive patent applications. The infrastructure and patent systems began operating in 2024. The 2026 amendment adjusts this existing framework in response to gaps revealed as it was implemented; it is not a new general foreign-investment screening law.

Supply-security policy had focused on the production and stockpiling of designated goods. The Cabinet Office's 2026 explanation identifies a blind spot: a specialist service indispensable to supplying a critical good may itself depend heavily on external providers or face disruption. The amendment allows such service dependencies to be addressed through the critical-goods framework, alongside best-efforts cooperation and targeted requests for information where supply continuity is at risk.

Medical digitalization has made shared clinical records, electronic prescriptions and online eligibility checks important to care delivery. Government materials explain that disruption to these functions could affect medical institutions and pharmacies. The amendment therefore makes specified medical activities eligible for critical-infrastructure designation; it does not automatically impose equipment-screening duties on every hospital.

The government also identifies overseas transport links, essential-service facilities and international deployment of important technologies as economic-security priorities. These projects may be strategically valuable while offering uncertain commercial returns. The amendment creates a ministerial certification route and a separate JBIC account that can use subordinated investment to mobilize private capital; it does not guarantee project financing.

Finally, economic-security risks cross departmental, technical and industry boundaries. The new public-private council and integrated research function seek to connect government and private-sector information and expertise. These institutional arrangements do not, by themselves, create a universal reporting duty for all businesses.

Legislative & Regulatory History

The government began formal work on economic-security legislation in 2021, and the original Act was enacted in 2022. Supply-chain and advanced-technology measures began first, followed by critical-infrastructure and patent systems in 2024. As these systems operated, government materials identified service dependencies, medical digital infrastructure and overseas projects as areas needing further policy tools.

The Cabinet submitted the amendment bill on 19 March 2026. The House of Representatives amended it to add a requirement for the government to examine further measures against threats to national security arising from economic activities. The House of Councillors passed the amended bill on 10 June, and it was promulgated as Act No. 38 of 2026 on 17 June. That parliamentary amendment indicates that the policy agenda was not considered exhausted by this Act.

JBIC announced commencement of relevant provisions on 18 September 2026 but explained that the new overseas-support business still needed budget measures and preparation. The public-private council adopted its rules on 1 October 2026. Different provisions have different commencement arrangements; the start of one institutional measure does not establish that every new requirement is in force.

Business Impact & Practical Considerations

A manufacturer whose designated critical good depends on a specialist service can examine that dependency separately from the physical product. Where the statutory designation and approved supply-security plan requirements are met, support may reach service infrastructure or technology. This is not an automatic subsidy for every subcontractor. Best-efforts cooperation and requests for information should not be presented as universal mandatory filings.

Healthcare providers and technology vendors should distinguish a medical activity becoming eligible for designation from an actual designation. The practical question is whether a particular service, provider and equipment arrangement falls within the implementing rules. The amendment alone does not subject every hospital's equipment procurement to prior review.

Sponsors of strategic overseas transport or technology projects may eventually have an additional public-finance channel, but ministerial certification and JBIC's operational readiness matter. The intended mechanism is to share risks and attract private capital, not to remove all commercial or regulatory risks. Businesses participating in the public-private council should separately identify any member-specific confidentiality and cooperation obligations.

Points to check

  • For a critical-goods supply chain, identify specialist services essential to delivery and compare their interruption risks with the actual designated goods and applicable ministerial supply-security policies.
  • For a medical project, identify the precise service, operator and equipment transaction before assessing whether a designation and implementing rules trigger advance notification and screening.
  • For an overseas project, test the statutory project category and ministerial certification criteria, and verify JBIC's dedicated-account operating status before assuming public finance in the funding plan.
  • For public-private council participation, distinguish member-specific information-sharing and secrecy obligations from duties, if any, applicable to non-members.
